Goal

The goal of the this project was to practice working with a physical prototypes, and to experiment with movement of the human body. The main goal was to develop the project into something that could be used for the benfit our understanding this movement, specifically that of the human foot. This project was also into the exploration of the reseearch through design methodlogy that I would use later in many other projects, due to its focus on prototyping.

Prototypes

The prototypes consits of multiple different designs that aim to create unique walking experince for testers. The reason for the differnece in design between prototypes is because it was focsued on exploration, rather than iteration. This lead to prototypes exploring different aspects of human locomotion, just as foot movment, balance, and resistence. This also extendeding to the size of prototypes depending on the its specific role to disrupt human movement.

Tall Getas

Inspired by geta slippers from Japan, this prototype was aming to see what increasing the height of the foot did to the waliing gait of a person. With this prototype being the only iteration, based of the small getas

Sleds

This prototype mainly focused on restricitng human movement by adding frontward facing weight and creating a unbalanced base. Based of actual sleds, this prototype effected an individuals by forcing them to walk in a single direction with sliding motion. The unbalanced nature of the prototype also added to this effect and forced them to take a larger gait when they walked.

Skies

This prototype similar to the sleds focused on providing a limited amount of surface area for a person to work with when being used. With it being long and narrow, the skies were diffcuilt for people to use, and rather than walking. This lead to wearings having to take large trudging steps, mainly due to the shape of the footware preventing them from taking normal steps.

Short Getas

the Short Getas were the first prototype developed and served as a simple trial run in order to get into the rythem of building protoypes and testing them out. The Short Getas were comparably easy to walk, when compared to the other prototypes, with many testers and non-testers wanting to try them out for their ease of use. This made it easy to test with them and to gather important data about waling and the testing enviorment as well.

Point Balance

This prototype was second hardest prototype to use, as it featured minimal surface area for testers to use. The goal of the prototype was to see if it was possible to reduce the balance of tester, in order to create a unique gait or walking pattern. Testers after trying to use the device eventually resorted to walking as if wearing high heals, which provided the nesscary stability for them to walk albeit slow and controled.

Rabbit

This prototype was the largest and the hardest to use due to the size and extended nature of was meant to be the ankle and shins. The overall weight also made it diffcuilt to take steps with, as the heavy prototype prevented users from taking proper steps. Out of the prototypes that were tested the Rabbit's were never able to get a succesful use, and due to the lack of time further development and iteration could be done. Additionally many imrpovments on the design could have been made such as chaning material, or changing shape.

Outcome

By intentionally disrupting normal human locomotion, this project successfully mapped how minimal changes in surface area, weight distribution, and height force the human body to instinctively adapt. Beyond the physical data gathered from user testing, the project validated the power of rapid, exploratory prototyping over immediate iteration. It highlighted how physical constraints can serve as a primary research tool to uncover hidden nuances in ergonomics and biomechanics.

Example of the 'Tall Getas' being use

Purpose

Embracing the "Research through Design" approach allowed me to treat failure as a data point. For instance, while the 'Rabbit' prototype was too restrictive to walk in, it provided crucial insights into the threshold where assistive or alterative footwear becomes a hindrance rather than a tool for exploration.

Ultimately, this project laid a foundational framework for my future design work. It taught me to look at physical interfaces not just as static objects, but as dynamic interventions that alter human behavior. The insights gained regarding user testing environments and rapid physical fabrication directly informed my approach to complex interactions in subsequent, larger-scale projects.
